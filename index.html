The problem

People own clothes they forget they own, and buy more because of it. Wardrobe apps exist to fix that and mostly die the same way: the user has to photograph every garment before the app does anything useful, and almost nobody finishes.

I wanted to know two things before writing a line of product code — is the problem felt strongly enough to act on, and can the technology actually deliver the promise on a mid-range Indian phone?

The parity matrix was filled by a separate reader per app, under one standing rule: UNKNOWN is the default, and 90 honest unknowns beat one invented tick. Five apps were excluded on purpose because nobody had opened them.

What the evidence said

1. The most-wanted feature was one nobody had built

Ranked across 1,463 requests, the top ask was a deeper taxonomy. Sixth was custom, user-created categories — and that turned out to be the whole ethnic-wear answer.

“When you add clothes to your closet gallery with photos you can have custom categories, I use it a lot because I wear a lot of Indian sarees.” Smart Closet · 5★ · 2016

The only satisfied ethnic-wear user in a decade of reviews did not get a saree category. She got permission to make one. Eight years later a Whering reviewer asked for exactly that fallback. The lesson is not “add saree” — it is “ship a taxonomy and let people extend it”.

“pls can you add ethic wear section… like Indian wear, kurti, lehenga… like koreans have hanbok..pls do consider” Acloset · 4★ · 2024

Acloset already models hanbok — a Korean garment, in a Korean app. The schema can carry non-Western clothing. They built it for their home market and stopped. A user noticed, and made the argument better than I could.

2. “AI” is both the ask and the objection

The same corpus wants on-device intelligence and resents being sold it.

WantedNot wanted
“only on device processing and autotagging with db export” “an app that focused on data and didn’t shove AI outfit ideas down my throat”
“it keeps your data on your devices rather than on the cloud” “‘AI-powered’ is a pretty big turn off… is there the option to turn it off?”

The distinction is specific, and it is a product decision. Auto-tagging is labour people want removed. A stylist volunteering outfits is an opinion they did not ask for. So: tagging is automatic, the stylist only answers when asked, and the off switch ships at the same time — not in a later release.

3. The crowd had already solved onboarding, and the answer was not gamification

UpvotesWhat people actually do
683“clean out one drawer at a time, and then stop for a couple of days”
108“screenshot your item so you don’t have to photograph it”
49“log the outfit as you wear them instead of taking a day off to log all your clothes”

So the onboarding stopped asking for five items and started asking for one drawer — and logging what you wore became a way to build the wardrobe, not just record it. Neither idea was mine.

A licence audit ran alongside it. Two models the plan depended on turned out to be unlicensed for commercial use, and were replaced before any code was written around them.

Where I was wrong

Four corrections I had to make to my own work. I think these matter more than the things I got right.

I divided when I should have read the architecture. I claimed a progressive review screen would show its first tile in ~1.4 s, by dividing 10.9 by 8. The cutout runs once per photograph, not once per garment — so every tile lands at the same instant. The arithmetic was fine; the pipeline it described did not exist. Withdrawn from three documents.
I repeated an unsourced number four times. “Most users quit after fewer than 20 items” had anchored the whole onboarding argument across three documents. It has no source. Withdrawn — and the only “20” the evidence does support is wardrobe size, not abandonment.
I said nobody asked for on-device AI. They had. True of the video transcripts, false of the comments — where the privacy finding ran the other way entirely.
I shipped twelve messages that said nothing. A tester reported “button clicks, nothing happens, no message”. The cause was one empty function: React Native Web’s alert is static alert(){}. Every error, every confirmation, discarded. It typechecks and it works on a phone — only an error path in a browser reveals it.

The decision

128 features were marked for launch, 85 of them in the first release, to be built by one person. The honest answer to “can one person build this?” was no.

So I built an instrument instead of a product: four screens, no AI model shipped, and a pass mark written down before the build — 40% of 15 testers acting twice in ten days. If that number is not met, the remaining 81 features are not worth building, and I will have found out in ten days instead of ten months.

Two of the four were cut on principle rather than effort: importing from a link needs network scraping, which would end the “nothing leaves your phone” guarantee that is the product’s only real moat.
